Part 1 · Background Reading

Debate 1

Steel, Oil, and the Question of How to Judge Them

After the Civil War, American industry grew faster than anyone had thought possible. Railroads tied the country together, and new methods let factories make steel and refine oil in enormous amounts. Soon a few businessmen controlled whole industries. John D. Rockefeller started the Standard Oil Company in Ohio in 1870 and kept buying or squeezing out rivals until his company refined most of the oil in the country. Andrew Carnegie, who was born in Scotland and came to the United States as a boy, built a giant steelmaking empire around Pittsburgh. In 1901 he sold it to the banker J. P. Morgan, who combined it with other firms to form the United States Steel Corporation.

These men won through size and through tough tactics. They joined competing companies into powerful combinations called trusts, demanded secret discounts from the railroads that smaller rivals could not get, and cut prices until competitors gave up. Their defenders said that large organizations were more efficient and that endless competition only wasted money. It is true that the new factories made goods cheaper and gave jobs to thousands of people. But workers often labored long hours for low pay in dangerous places, and farmers and small shop owners complained that the giants pushed them aside. Reformers and writers began to ask whether these fortunes had been earned fairly.

The tycoons and their critics told very different stories. In 1889 Carnegie published an essay called “Wealth,” later known as the “Gospel of Wealth.” He argued that great inequality was the price of progress, and that rich people had a duty to use their fortunes for the public good while they were still alive. Ida Tarbell was a journalist who spent years investigating Standard Oil for McClure’s Magazine. In 1905 she published a character study of Rockefeller that accused him of corrupt dealing and of crushing competition. The two documents you will read next come from these two people.

Historians still disagree about how to judge such men. Matthew Josephson called them “robber barons,” businessmen who built their fortunes mainly for personal gain by ruthless means. Allan Nevins thought that label unfair. He saw “captains of industry,” innovators who organized the economy and made products cheaper. In 1958 Hal Bridges argued that “robber baron” was too simple a label, and Harold Livesay concluded that the great industrialists were both: creative and ambitious, but also ruthless and ready to exploit others. As you read, ask whether each writer’s evidence supports one side or both.

Debate 2

Who Belongs? Newcomers and the Nation

In the decades after 1880, many millions of people crossed the oceans to live in the United States. Earlier immigrants had come mostly from Britain, Ireland, Germany, and Scandinavia. Now most came from southern and eastern Europe, including Italians, Russians, Poles, Hungarians, and Greeks, while Chinese workers arrived on the Pacific coast. They came for jobs in mines, mills, and workshops, and many followed relatives who had already settled. Ellis Island, the inspection station in New York harbor, opened in 1892. Many newcomers spoke languages that most Americans could not understand, and many were Catholic or Jewish, so Americans argued about whether they could ever fit in. Many native-born workers also worried that the newcomers would take jobs and push wages down.

The hostility began on the West Coast. Chinese laborers had come to California for the gold rush and to build railroads. When wages fell in the 1870s, many white workers blamed them. In 1877 the Workingmen’s Party of California, whose officers included Denis Kearney and H. L. Knight, campaigned against Chinese labor. Congress responded with the Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882, which banned Chinese laborers for ten years, and the Geary Act of 1892 kept the ban going. Some Americans objected. In 1893 the lawyer and speaker Robert Ingersoll took part in a magazine debate and argued against excluding the Chinese.

Nativism is the belief that people already born in a country deserve preference over newcomers. In the 1890s it shaped the debate over the new arrivals from Europe. Senator Henry Cabot Lodge of Massachusetts spoke in 1896 for a bill that would require immigrants to pass a reading test, hoping to keep out many of the poorest newcomers. Congress passed a bill like it in 1897, but President Grover Cleveland vetoed it. Meanwhile, many immigrants were building lives of their own. In 1902 a magazine printed the life story of Rocco Corresca, an Italian immigrant who shined shoes in New York City.

Historians have given three answers to the question “What happened to the newcomers?” Frederick Jackson Turner, Marcus Lee Hansen, and Oscar Handlin described a “melting pot” in which immigrants were gradually Americanized. Madison Grant in 1916 and John Higham in 1955 stressed nativism, the fear and rejection of newcomers. Frank Thistlethwaite, Alan Kraut, and John Bodnar pictured a “tossed salad”: immigrants moved back and forth across the ocean, relied on family ties, and kept parts of their old cultures while also becoming Americans.

DEBATE 1 · CASE FILES 1–2

Robber Barons or Captains of Industry?

The debate:

Were the great industrialists of the Gilded Age “robber barons” who built fortunes by exploiting others, or “captains of industry” whose vision and organization built the modern economy?

  • ★ ROBBER BARONS: Matthew Josephson argued that the great industrialists were driven mainly by personal gain and used ruthless methods to win.
  • ◆ CAPTAINS OF INDUSTRY: Allan Nevins argued that “robber baron” is an unfair label: these men were innovators who organized industry and lowered prices.
  • ● BOTH / MIXED: Harold Livesay argued that they were creative and ambitious but also ruthless and willing to exploit others. Hal Bridges (1958) agreed that “robber baron” alone is not enough.
1889Case file 1 of 6
Who: Andrew Carnegie, steel manufacturer
From: Essay, “Wealth” (later called the “Gospel of Wealth”)

The steel maker who had become one of the richest men in America explains what rich people owe to everyone else.

The problem of our age is the administration of wealth, so that the ties of brotherhood may still bind together the rich and poor in harmonious relationship. . . . This change, however, is not to be deplored, but welcomed as highly beneficial. . . . Much better this great irregularity than universal squalor. . . . This, then, is held to be the duty of the man of Wealth: . . . the man of wealth thus becoming the sole agent and trustee for his poorer brethren, bringing to their service his superior wisdom, experience, and ability to administer—doing for them better than they would or could do for themselves.
Andrew Carnegie, “Wealth,” North American Review 148, no. 391 (June 1889): 653–64, Fordham University Internet Modern History Sourcebook. Fordham and the Norton exercise print a hyphen in “administer-doing”; a dash is shown here.
Word help: administration = management · ties of brotherhood = bonds that hold people together · deplored = regretted · squalor = filthy, miserable poverty · trustee = a person who manages property for the benefit of others · brethren = brothers

1905Case file 2 of 6
Who: Ida Tarbell, journalist
From: Magazine article, “John D. Rockefeller: A Character Study,” part two

A reporter who investigated Standard Oil sums up her charges against its founder.

He has turned commerce from a peaceful pursuit to war, and honeycombed it with cruel and corrupt practice; turned competition from honorable emulation to cutthroat struggle. . . . Cheap oil? Mr. Rockefeller’s fundamental reason for forming his first combination was to keep up the price of oil. It has been forced down by the inventions and discoveries of his competitors.
Ida M. Tarbell, “John D. Rockefeller: A Character Study,” part two, McClure’s Magazine 25, no. 4 (August 1905): 396–97, Internet Archive scan. The Norton exercise gives a different issue date; the scan shows August 1905.
Word help: honeycombed = filled with holes · emulation = friendly rivalry · cutthroat = ruthless · combination = a joining of companies into one

DEBATE 2 · CASE FILES 3–6

Melting Pot, Nativism, or Tossed Salad?

The debate:

When millions of new immigrants arrived, did the United States turn them into Americans (a “melting pot”), reject them (nativism), or become a mix in which newcomers kept parts of their old ways (a “tossed salad”)?

  • ★ MELTING POT: Frederick Jackson Turner, Marcus Lee Hansen, and Oscar Handlin argued that immigrants could be Americanized, so the nation melted many peoples into one.
  • ◆ NATIVISM: Madison Grant (1916) feared that the new immigrants would weaken the nation, and John Higham (1955) showed how fear and hostility toward newcomers shaped American attitudes and laws.
  • ● BOTH / MIXED: Frank Thistlethwaite (1960), Alan Kraut, and John Bodnar argued for a “tossed salad”: immigrants kept parts of their cultures, went back and forth across the ocean, and stayed linked to their homelands while also becoming American.
1878Case file 3 of 6
Who: Denis Kearney and H. L. Knight, officers of the Workingmen’s Party of California
From: Public address, “Appeal from California. The Chinese Invasion. Workingmen’s Address”

Two labor leaders in California tell the nation what they think of Chinese workers.

To add to our misery and despair, a bloated aristocracy has sent to China—the greatest and oldest despotism in the world—for a cheap working slave. It rakes the slums of Asia to find the meanest slave on earth . . . and imports him here to meet the free American in the Labor market, and still further widen the breach between the rich and the poor, still further to degrade white Labor. . . . California must be all American or all Chinese. We are resolved that it shall be American, and are prepared to make it so.
Denis Kearney and H. L. Knight, “Appeal from California. The Chinese Invasion. Workingmen’s Address,” Indianapolis Times, February 28, 1878, in Who Built America?, American Social History Project, CUNY.
Word help: aristocracy = a small, rich ruling class · despotism = rule by one all-powerful tyrant · breach = gap · degrade = lower the standing of · resolved = firmly determined

1893Case file 4 of 6
Who: Robert G. Ingersoll, lawyer and public speaker
From: Magazine debate, “Should the Chinese Be Excluded?”

A well-known speaker answers the argument that the Chinese should be kept out of the United States.

In our own country the native has always considered himself as much better than the immigrant, and as far superior to all people of a different complexion. At one time our people hated the Irish, then the Germans, then the Italians, and now the Chinese. . . . The Irish and Germans improved their condition. They went into other businesses, into the higher and more lucrative trades. They entered the professions, turned their attention to politics, became merchants, brokers, and professors in colleges. . . . Our country is an asylum for the oppressed of all nations—of all races.
Robert G. Ingersoll, in “Should the Chinese Be Excluded?” (a debate with Representative Geary), North American Review 157 (July 1893): 52–58, Internet Archive scan, checked against infidels.org.

1896Case file 5 of 6
Who: Henry Cabot Lodge, U.S. Senator from Massachusetts
From: Senate speech, “The Restriction of Immigration,” March 16, 1896

A senator argues that the country should limit the flow of newcomers from southern and eastern Europe.

[O]ther races of totally different race origin, with whom the English-speaking people have never hitherto been assimilated or brought in contact, have suddenly begun to immigrate to the United States in large numbers. Russians, Hungarians, Poles, Bohemians, Italians, Greeks, and even Asiatics, whose immigration to America was almost unknown twenty years ago, have during the last twenty years poured in in steadily increasing numbers . . . . It involves, in a word, nothing less than the possibility of a great and perilous change in the very fabric of our race. . . . The time has certainly come, if not to stop, at least to check, to sift, and to restrict those immigrants.
Henry Cabot Lodge, “The Restriction of Immigration,” Senate speech, March 16, 1896, in Speeches and Addresses, 1884–1909 (Boston: Houghton Mifflin, 1909), 245–66. The Norton exercise dates the speech 1909, the year of the book, and prints “to, sift”; the book prints “to sift.”
Word help: assimilated = absorbed into a group and made alike · Asiatics = people from Asia (an outdated term) · perilous = dangerous · fabric = the basic structure · sift = sort through to keep some and remove others · restrict = limit

Who: Rocco Corresca, an Italian immigrant who shined shoes in New York City
From: Life story, “The Biography of a Bootblack”

A young immigrant tells how his plans have changed since he arrived.

We had said that when we saved $1,000 each we would go back to Italy and buy a farm, but now that the time is coming we are so busy and making so much money that we think we will stay. . . . I like this country now and I don’t see why we should have a king. . . . I and Francisco are to be Americans in three years. The court gave us papers and said we must wait and we must be able to read some things and tell who the ruler of the country is. . . . We have joined a club and have much pleasure in the evenings.
