Part 1 · Background Reading

Debate 1

Settlers, Exodusters, and a Famous Essay

After the Civil War, Americans moved west in huge numbers. Railroads carried settlers, miners, and ranchers across the plains, and the Homestead Act of 1862 offered 160 acres of public land to any family willing to farm it. But the land was not empty. Native nations had lived there for centuries, and the U.S. Army fought many wars to force them onto reservations. The Nez Perce, a people of the Pacific Northwest, were driven from their homeland in the 1870s. Their leader, Chief Joseph, surrendered in 1877 and traveled to Washington in 1879 to ask for justice. Miners rushed to the mountains in search of gold, and ranchers pushed cattle onto the plains, so the arrival of settlers often meant the loss of land for the people already there.

Other Americans went west to escape hardship. After Reconstruction ended in 1877, many Black families in the South faced violence, poverty, and little hope of owning land. In 1879 thousands of them left for Kansas. They were called Exodusters, after the biblical Exodus. Benjamin Singleton, an African American who had helped organize colonies of Black settlers, testified about the movement to a U.S. Senate committee in 1880.

Farm families who settled on western land soon found that success was hard. They paid for machinery, seed, and railroad shipping, borrowed money, and depended on crop prices that they could not control. In 1890 the Census Bureau reported that the frontier line, the edge of settlement, no longer existed. Three years later, at a meeting of historians in Chicago, the historian Frederick Jackson Turner argued that the frontier had made Americans who they were. His essay became one of the most famous in U.S. history. In 1905 a farm woman described her own days in a magazine article without giving her name. Women on farms worked in the house and in the fields, and most of what they earned went back into the farm.

Historians still argue about the West. Turner said that free land gave Americans freedom, equality, and a new democratic character. He believed that each time settlers met a new land, they dropped old European habits and became more independent and practical. He described the frontier as the meeting point between savagery and civilization. The New Western historians answered that this story left people out. Patricia Nelson Limerick (1987) described the West as a place where different peoples competed to control land, water, and resources, so that conquest was central to its story, Nell Irvin Painter (1980) told the story of the Exodusters, and Quintard Taylor (1998) described a region shaped by peoples of many backgrounds. As you read, ask whether each source fits Turner’s frontier, the New Western story, or parts of both.

Debate 2

A Prize in the Pacific

In 1898 the United States went to war with Spain, mainly over Cuba. The war lasted only a few months, but its results lasted much longer. A U.S. fleet destroyed the Spanish fleet at Manila Bay in the Philippines, and in the Treaty of Paris that year the United States bought the islands from Spain. McKinley’s negotiators decided to keep the entire island group, and the Senate narrowly approved the treaty in early 1899. Many Filipinos, who had been fighting Spain for their own independence, did not accept a new ruler. In 1899 fighting broke out between U.S. troops and Filipino forces, and the war dragged on for years.

Americans argued fiercely about what to do. Business leaders hoped the islands would be a gateway to Asia, especially to the huge market of China, after the depression of the 1890s had made them hunt for new buyers for American goods. Others spoke of duty. They said that Americans must uplift and guide peoples they considered less advanced, a belief mixed with the racial ideas of the time. Supporters also warned that if the United States left, a rival such as Germany or Japan might take the islands. Critics of annexation answered that a republic should not rule other people without their consent.

The case files come from the leaders in that debate. President William McKinley explained his decision in a message to Congress in December 1899. In January and March 1900, Senators Albert Beveridge and Henry Cabot Lodge made speeches in the Senate about keeping the islands. In 1901 Theodore Roosevelt, who had fought in Cuba in 1898 and was then vice president, spoke to a crowd at the Minnesota State Fair about the nation’s “duties.” Each man had a different job and a different audience, so notice who is speaking and why as well as what is said.

Historians disagree about the motives behind these words. Nell Irvin Painter (1989) stressed economic motives, arguing that the pull of overseas markets and business interests helped drive the decision. Kristin L. Hoganson (1998) stressed cultural and gender motives, arguing that political leaders used ideas about manhood to make the case for empire and to show that the nation was strong. Both historians agree that the leaders spoke in confident language, but they disagree about which motive mattered more. As you read, ask whether a speaker is talking mostly about money, about manhood and duty, or about both.

DEBATE 1 · CASE FILES 1–4

The West: Myth or Reality?

The debate:

Was the American West a land of free land and opportunity that shaped the American character, or a place of conquest, conflict, and many peoples whose stories the older myth left out?

  • ★ TURNER’S FRONTIER: Frederick Jackson Turner argued in 1893 that the frontier and its free land shaped American democracy and the American character.
  • ◆ NEW WESTERN HISTORY: Patricia Nelson Limerick (1987), Nell Irvin Painter (1980), and Quintard Taylor (1998) argued that the West was a place of conquest, conflict, and meetings among many peoples, whose stories Turner left out.
  • ● BOTH / MIXED: A source whose evidence fits parts of both arguments: it shows people seeking land and opportunity and also meeting hardship or exclusion.
1879Case file 1 of 8
Who: Chief Joseph, leader of the Nez Perce
From: Article, “An Indian’s Views of Indian Affairs”

A Native leader tells American readers what happened to his people when white settlers came.

White men had found gold in the mountains around the land of winding water. They stole a great many horses from us, and we could not get them back because we were Indians. The white men told lies for each other. They drove off a great many of our cattle. . . . We had no friend who would plead our cause before the law councils. . . . If we ever owned the land we own it still, for we never sold it. . . . I know that my race must change. We can not hold our own with the white men as we are. We only ask an even chance to live as other men live.
Chief Joseph, “An Indian’s Views of Indian Affairs,” North American Review 128, no. 269 (April 1879), Internet Archive scan. The Norton exercise prints a later version credited to Fee (1936) whose wording differs; the 1879 magazine text is used here.
Word help: plead our cause = argue our case · law councils = courts and lawmakers · race = people

A Black organizer tells senators why families left the South for Kansas.

Well, my people, for the want of land—we needed land for our children—and their disadvantages—that caused my heart to grieve and sorrow; pity for my race, sir, that was coming down, instead of going up—that caused me to go to work for them. . . . 1893Case file 3 of 8
Who: Frederick Jackson Turner, historian
From: Address, “The Significance of the Frontier in American History”

A historian explains how he thinks the frontier made Americans.

In this advance, the frontier is the outer edge of the wave—the meeting point between savagery and civilization. . . . The most significant thing about the American frontier is, that it lies at the hither edge of free land. . . . In short, at the frontier the environment is at first too strong for the man. He must accept the conditions which it furnishes, or perish . . . . Little by little he transforms the wilderness, but the outcome is not the old Europe . . . . DEBATE 2 · CASE FILES 5–8

Why Annex the Philippines?

The debate:

Why did the United States take and keep the Philippines after the Spanish-American War: to win markets and trade (economic motives), or to prove national manhood and spread American culture (cultural and gender motives)?

  • ★ ECONOMIC MOTIVES: Nell Irvin Painter argued in “The White Man’s Burden” (1989) that the search for overseas markets and the interests of business drove the decision.
  • ◆ CULTURE AND MANHOOD: Kristin L. Hoganson argued in “The National Manhood Metaphor” (1998) that ideas about manhood, duty, and racial superiority pushed leaders to prove the nation’s strength abroad.
  • ● BOTH / MIXED: A source whose evidence fits both arguments at once: it talks about markets and about duty or manliness.
1899Case file 5 of 8
Who: William McKinley, President of the United States
From: Third Annual Message to Congress, December 5, 1899

The president says what would happen if the United States gave up the Philippine Islands, and what Americans will do there.

If we desert them we leave them at once to anarchy and finally to barbarism. We fling them, a golden apple of discord, among the rival powers, no one of which could permit another to seize them unquestioned. . . . We shall continue, as we have begun, to open the schools and the churches, to set the courts in operation, to foster industry and trade and agriculture, and in every way in our power to make these people whom Providence has brought within our jurisdiction feel that it is their liberty and not our power, their welfare and not our gain, we are seeking to enhance.
William McKinley, Third Annual Message to Congress, December 5, 1899, The Miller Center, University of Virginia. The Norton exercise credits Foreign Relations of the United States, 1899.
Word help: title = legal right · equity = fairness · anarchy = lawlessness · barbarism = a biased word for a lack of civilization · golden apple of discord = a prize that causes quarrels · Providence = God’s plan · jurisdiction = authority · enhance = increase

1900Case file 6 of 8
Who: Albert J. Beveridge, U.S. Senator from Indiana
From: Senate speech, “Our Philippine Policy,” January 9, 1900

A senator says whether the Filipino people should govern themselves.

But, Senators, it would be better to abandon the Philippines, and count our blood and treasure already spent a profitable loss, than to apply any academic arrangement of self-government to these children. They are not yet capable of self-government. How could they be? They are not a self-governing race; they are Orientals, Malays, instructed by Spaniards in the latter’s worst estate. . . . Self-government is no cheap boon, to be bestowed on the merely audacious. It is the degree which crowns the graduate of liberty, not the reward of liberty’s infant class, which has not yet mastered the alphabet of freedom.

Who: Theodore Roosevelt, Vice President of the United States
From: Speech, “National Duties,” Minnesota State Fair, September 2, 1901

A war hero turned vice president tells a fair crowd what Americans owe the Philippines.

Let me insist again . . . upon the fact that our duty is twofold, and that we must raise others while we are benefiting ourselves. . . .
