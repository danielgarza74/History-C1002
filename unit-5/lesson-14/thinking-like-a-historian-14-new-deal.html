Part 1 · Background Reading

Action, Experiment, and the Limits of Relief

When the stock market crashed in October 1929, few people guessed that the country was heading into its worst economic crisis. Over the next three years banks failed, businesses closed, and farm prices collapsed. By 1933 roughly one worker in four had no job, and families who had never asked for help stood in line for bread. President Herbert Hoover believed that private charity and local relief should carry most of the burden, and many Americans felt abandoned. In 1932 they elected Franklin D. Roosevelt, who promised “a new deal for the American people.”

Roosevelt acted at once. In his first hundred days Congress passed laws to reopen the banks, to put young men to work on public lands in the Civilian Conservation Corps, to pay farmers to grow less, to build dams and power plants in the Tennessee Valley, and to set up codes of fair competition for industry. The government now regulated banking, helped fund relief, and took a much larger part in the economy. In 1935 came a second wave: the Social Security Act created old-age pensions and unemployment insurance, the Wagner Act protected the right of workers to join unions, and the Works Progress Administration paid millions of people to build roads, schools, and parks. Not everyone cheered. Business leaders warned that Roosevelt was going too far. Others thought he was not going far enough, among them Senator Huey Long of Louisiana, who called for a plan to “share our wealth” by limiting great fortunes. Roosevelt was reelected in a landslide in 1936, but unemployment stayed high, and some of the new programs, such as Social Security, left out farm and household workers, jobs that many Black Americans held, while Southern Democrats in Congress held great power.

Historians have argued for decades about how much the New Deal really changed. Henry Steele Commager and Arthur Schlesinger Jr. belonged to what is called the progressive school. They saw Roosevelt as heir to earlier reformers who used government to curb big business, and they argued that the New Deal kept the American system while adjusting it. Richard Hofstadter, writing in the 1950s, agreed that it was a break with the past but described it as timid and practical, a set of experiments rather than a plan. William Leuchtenburg, writing in 1963, insisted that it was a “radically new departure,” because the federal government took on lasting responsibility for the economic security of its citizens. Even so, he called it a “halfway revolution,” since it did not remake the economy and left African Americans and others outside many of its protections. More recent social historians, who study workers, farmers, women, and Black Americans, tend to find a mixed bag of real help and real limits.

Three voices will test these views. In 1934 Roosevelt explained his program to the nation in one of his radio “fireside chats.” In January 1935 Huey Long attacked the president in the Senate. In 1940 Roy Wilkins, a Black leader and editor of the NAACP magazine The Crisis, weighed Roosevelt’s record on race. Ask of each source: whose argument does this evidence help most?

DEBATE 1 · CASE FILES 1–3

The New Deal: Continuation or Halfway Revolution?

The debate:

Was the New Deal a “halfway revolution” that changed the role of government but left many Americans behind, or a continuation of earlier reform that kept the existing order in place?

  • ★ CONTINUATION: Henry Steele Commager and Arthur Schlesinger Jr., of the “progressive school,” argued that the New Deal modified rather than replaced the existing order and carried earlier reform forward.
  • ◆ HALFWAY REVOLUTION: William Leuchtenburg called the New Deal a “radically new departure” for government, yet a “halfway revolution” that left many, especially African Americans, behind.
  • ● BOTH / MIXED: A source that fits both arguments. Richard Hofstadter saw a real departure that was also timid and pragmatic, and recent social historians find a mixed bag of real help and real limits.
1934Case file 1 of 3
Who: President Franklin D. Roosevelt
From: Radio address, a “fireside chat,” September 30, 1934

The president explains to listeners why his program does not break with American tradition.

To those who say that our expenditures for Public Works and other means for recovery are a waste that we cannot afford, I answer that no country, however rich, can afford the waste of its human resources. Demoralization caused by vast unemployment is our greatest extravagance. . . . The course we have followed fits the American practice of Government - a practice of taking action step by step, of regulating only to meet concrete needs - a practice of courageous recognition of change.
Franklin D. Roosevelt, fireside chat of September 30, 1934, Social Security Administration, History Pages (ssa.gov/history/fdrstmts.html). The spaced hyphens are in the source.
Word help: expenditures = money spent · Public Works = government building projects such as roads and dams · demoralization = loss of hope and spirit · extravagance = wasteful spending · concrete = real and specific

1935Case file 2 of 3
Who: Senator Huey Long of Louisiana
From: Speech in the U.S. Senate, “Share Our Wealth,” January 14, 1935

A senator who once backed the president says the New Deal leaves the problem of wealth untouched.

One million more unemployed; 5 million more families on the dole, and another 5 million trying to get there; the fortunes of the rich becoming bigger and the fortunes of the average and little men getting less and less; the money in the banks nearly all owned by a mere handful of people, and the President of the United States quoted as saying: “Don't touch the rich!” . . . So therefore I call upon the men and women of America to immediately join in our work and movement to share our wealth.
Huey P. Long, speech in the U.S. Senate, January 14, 1935, Social Security Administration, History Pages (ssa.gov/history/longsen.html).
Word help: dole = money paid by the government to people who have no work · fortunes = great amounts of wealth · a mere handful = only a very few

1940Case file 3 of 3
Who: Roy Wilkins, NAACP leader and editor of The Crisis
From: Editorial, “The Roosevelt Record,” The Crisis (edited by Roy Wilkins), November 1940

A Black editor looks back on what Roosevelt’s program did and did not do for Black Americans.

On the subject of the Negro, the Roosevelt record is spotty, as might be expected in an administration where so much power is in the hands of the southern wing of the Democratic party. And yet Mr. Roosevelt, hobbled as he has been by the Dixie die-hards, has managed to include Negro citizens in practically every phase of the administration program. . . . For the first time in their lives, government has taken on meaning and substance for the Negro masses.
“The Roosevelt Record,” unsigned editorial, The Crisis (Roy Wilkins, editor), November 1940, p. 343; Norton credits the piece to Wilkins. The word “Negro” is the source’s term for Black Americans in 1940.
Word help: spotty = uneven, good in some places and bad in others · hobbled = held back · die-hards = people who will not change their minds · Negro = the term used in 1940 for Black Americans; it is outdated today
