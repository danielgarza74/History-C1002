Part 1 · Background Reading

A Movement for Everyone, or for Some?

Between about 1890 and 1920, reformers across the United States set out to repair the problems that factories, big cities, and giant corporations had created. Historians call them progressives because they had an unusual faith in progress, the belief that American life could keep getting better. They trusted facts and expert knowledge: investigators gathered data, and reformers used it to argue for new laws. They worked wherever they could, in city halls, state legislatures, and finally Washington, trying to bring order, stability, and what they called social justice to a society that seemed to be spinning out of control.

Historians have never agreed on who the progressives really were. Richard Hofstadter described them as old elites whose status was shaken by industrial change. Others pictured corporate leaders using regulation to push out competitors, or a movement of workers against special interests, or a movement shaped by women reformers. In 1970 Peter Filene went so far as to declare the idea of one progressive movement “dead,” since the reformers and their causes were too varied. Michael McGerr offered a sharper answer: progressives were mostly white, middle-class people who wanted to remake society in their own image by ending class conflict and regulating big business.

That last point leads to the question of this assignment. In 1954 Rayford Logan called the years after Reconstruction the nadir of American race relations, and the Progressive Era fell right inside that low point. Segregation spread, Black voters were pushed out of politics, and mobs lynched Black Americans with little fear of punishment. McGerr and David Southern argued that most white progressives either ignored these injustices or even supported segregation as a reform, and a few, such as Jane Addams and Lillian Wald, who spoke out against racial prejudice, were the exceptions. Another group of scholars looks at the same years from a different angle. Paula Giddings, Glenda Gilmore, and Kevin Gaines emphasize that Black leaders such as Ida B. Wells, Mary Church Terrell, Booker T. Washington, and W. E. B. Du Bois fought discrimination in work, schooling, law, and politics. Gilmore stressed that when white reformers shut Black Americans out, Black women built their own progressive projects in education, public health, and community improvement, working through churches, clubs, schools, and the Black press.

Four voices will let you test these arguments. In 1905 W. E. B. Du Bois and about thirty other Black leaders met near Niagara Falls and founded the Niagara Movement, which later grew into the National Association for the Advancement of Colored People (NAACP). At a 1906 meeting at Harpers Ferry, West Virginia, the site of John Brown’s raid, Du Bois spoke for it. In 1909 Ida B. Wells, who had made her name by investigating lynching, addressed the National Negro Conference in New York, the meeting that led to the NAACP’s founding. In 1912 Theodore Roosevelt, a former president who was challenging President William Howard Taft for the Republican nomination, explained what a progressive should stand for. Woodrow Wilson then won the White House in 1912 on a progressive program, backed by some Black leaders who trusted his promise of fair treatment. Yet segregation spread in federal offices under his administration, and in November 1914 William Monroe Trotter, a Black newspaper editor in Boston, led a delegation to the White House to protest it. Ask of each source: whose argument does this evidence help most?

DEBATE 1 · CASE FILES 1–4

Progressivism and Racism: How Progressive Was It?

The debate:

Did white progressives mostly ignore or harm Black Americans in the name of reform, or did Black progressives build their own reform movement that kept the fight for justice alive?

  • ★ WHITE REFORMERS FELL SHORT: Michael McGerr and David Southern saw white progressives as uninterested in racial justice and often hostile to it; Rayford Logan (1954) called the era the “nadir,” or lowest point, of American race relations.
  • ◆ BLACK PROGRESSIVES LED: Paula Giddings, Glenda Gilmore, and Kevin Gaines showed that, despite white hostility, many Black progressives tackled discrimination and built their own reform networks.
  • ● BOTH / MIXED: A source that fits both arguments: it speaks of progressive ideals and also shows the racial blind spot, or it helps both groups of historians.
1906Case file 1 of 4
Who: W. E. B. Du Bois, scholar and leader of the Niagara Movement
From: Speech, “Address to the Country,” Harpers Ferry, West Virginia

A Black leader tells the nation what his organization demands, on the ground made famous by John Brown.

We claim for ourselves every single right that belongs to a freeborn American, political, civil, and social; and until we get these rights we will never cease to protest and assail the ears of America. The battle we wage is not for ourselves alone but for all true Americans. . . . We want full manhood suffrage, and we want it now, henceforth, and forever. Second. We want discrimination in public accommodation to cease. Separation in railway and street cars, based simply on race and color, is un-American, un-democratic, and silly.
W. E. B. Du Bois, “Address to the Country,” speech at Harpers Ferry, West Virginia, August 19, 1906, The Broad Ax (Chicago), August 25, 1906, p. 1, via Teaching American History, Ashbrook Center. Norton’s credit is The New York Times, August 20, 1906; its text reads “henceforth and forever,” without the second comma. The Broad Ax wording is used here.
Word help: freeborn = born free, not enslaved · suffrage = the right to vote · manhood suffrage = voting rights for men (Du Bois’s phrase in 1906) · public accommodation = places open to the public, such as trains and hotels · un-democratic = against democracy

1909Case file 2 of 4
Who: Ida B. Wells, anti-lynching journalist and civil rights activist
From: Speech, “Lynching, Our National Crime,” National Negro Conference, New York

A journalist who has studied lynching for years gives a conference her findings and her remedy.

The lynching record for a quarter of a century merits the thoughtful study of the American people. It presents three salient facts: First: Lynching is color line murder. Second: Crimes against women is the excuse, not the cause. Third: It is a national crime and requires a national remedy. . . . The only certain remedy is an appeal to law. Lawbreakers must be made to know that human life is sacred and that every citizen of this country is first a citizen of the United States and secondly a citizen of the state in which he belongs.
Ida B. Wells-Barnett, “Lynching, Our National Crime,” in Proceedings of the National Negro Conference 1909 (New York, May 31 and June 1, 1909), pp. 174–177, Internet Archive full text. Norton’s printing continues a few words past this point, so this excerpt stops one sentence earlier.
Word help: salient = most important · color line = the dividing line between Black and white Americans · remedy = a cure or fix · federal = of the national government · lawlessness = ignoring the law

1912Case file 3 of 4
Who: Theodore Roosevelt, former president and candidate for the Republican nomination
From: Speech, “Who Is a Progressive?”

A leading progressive explains what it means to stand for “social justice.”

Every man is to that extent a Progressive if he stands for any form of social justice . . . for securing a proper limitation of hours of labor for women and children in industry, for securing proper living conditions for those who dwell in the thickly crowded regions of our great cities . . . . This country will not be a good place for any of us to live in if it is not a reasonably good place for all of us to live in. . . . Our cause is the cause of justice for all, in the interest of all.
Theodore Roosevelt, “Who Is a Progressive?” (1912), in Social Justice and Popular Rule: Essays, Addresses, and Public Statements Relating to the Progressive Movement (1910–1916) (New York: Charles Scribner’s Sons, 1926), text from Teaching American History, Ashbrook Center. A typo in the printed list (“whether it securing”) is skipped with an ellipsis.
Word help: social justice = fair treatment and fair chances for everyone · hours of labor = how long people must work in a day · thickly crowded regions = packed city neighborhoods

1914Case file 4 of 4
Who: Monroe Trotter, Black newspaper editor, and President Woodrow Wilson
From: Conversation at the White House, as printed in The Crisis

A delegation of Black leaders protests a new policy to the president who promised them fair treatment.

Mr. Monroe Trotter. Mr. President, we are here to renew our protest against the segregation of colored employes in the departments of our National Government. . . . President Woodrow Wilson. The white people of the country, the President continued, as well as I, wish to see the colored people progress, and admire the progress they have already made, and want to see them continue along independent lines. There is, however, a great prejudice against colored people. . . . Segregation is not humiliating, but a benefit, and ought to be so regarded by you gentlemen.
Exchange between Woodrow Wilson and Monroe Trotter, November 1914, “Opinion,” The Crisis, January 1915, pp. 119–120, as printed in the Norton exercise. Spelling and “colored” are the source’s.
Word help: segregation = forced separation of people by race · colored = a term for Black Americans used at the time · prejudice = an unfair opinion held without good reason · independent lines = separate, not shared
