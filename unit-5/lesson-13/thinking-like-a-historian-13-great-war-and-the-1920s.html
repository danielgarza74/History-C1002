Part 1 · Background Reading

Debate 1

From Neutrality to War

When war broke out in Europe in the summer of 1914, President Woodrow Wilson asked Americans to be neutral in thought and in deed, and for nearly three years the United States stayed out. Neutrality was hard to keep. Britain and France, known as the Allies, set up a blockade that kept American ships from German ports, and American banks lent hundreds of millions of dollars to the Allies. Germany answered with submarines. In May 1915 a German submarine sank the British passenger liner Lusitania, and Americans were among the dead. Germany then promised to limit its attacks, and in 1916 Wilson won a second term with a campaign that stressed keeping the country at peace. But in early 1917 Germany announced that its submarines would sink ships in a war zone without warning, and in the weeks that followed American merchant ships were attacked. Soon afterward the British showed American officials an intercepted German message, the Zimmermann telegram, which proposed that Mexico join Germany in a war against the United States. On April 2, 1917, Wilson asked Congress to declare war, and Congress voted for war a few days later, with only a small number of members voting no.

Historians have offered three main explanations. Arthur Link and Ross Kennedy stress national security. In their view Germany’s submarine campaign and its attempt to draw in Mexico threatened American lives, American ships, and the safety of the nation, and Wilson had to respond. Critics answer that Britain’s blockade also broke the rules for neutral ships, yet no one asked for war against Britain. Charles Beard and Hartley Grattan stress economic self-interest. They argued that loans and trade tied the country to an Allied victory, so bankers and manufacturers stood to lose if Germany won. Ernest May stresses moral duty. He argued that Wilson believed the United States had a responsibility to defend democratic values and to help build a lasting peace, an idea Wilson summed up in the phrase “make the world safe for democracy.”

Three voices will let you test these arguments. In his war message to Congress on April 2, 1917, Wilson explained why he thought the country must fight. Two members of Congress, Senators Robert La Follette and George Norris, spoke against war on April 4 as the Senate prepared to vote. Each had a different reason for his doubts, and each case file may help a different historian. Notice also who is speaking and when: Wilson was making a request to a nation that had stayed out of the war for almost three years, while the two senators were speaking in the last hours before the vote. Ask of each source: whose argument does this evidence help most?

Debate 2

The Scandals and the Second Look

Warren G. Harding, a newspaper publisher and senator from Ohio, won the presidency in 1920 with a call for a calmer, quieter government after the strains of the Great War. Voters were tired of the war years and of strong calls to reform the world, and Harding promised a return to what he called normalcy. He took office in March 1921, and in his inaugural address he asked Americans to expect less from government and to put the nation’s finances in order. He died suddenly in August 1923, in the middle of his term. After his death, stories spread of corruption among officials he had appointed. The best known is the Teapot Dome affair, in which the government’s oil reserves for the Navy were leased to private oil men in return for favors, with the approval of the secretary of the interior, Albert Fall, one of Harding’s own cabinet choices. Harding himself was not shown to have profited, but the scandals damaged his reputation for good.

In 1948 the historian Arthur Schlesinger Sr. asked a group of scholars to rank the presidents, and Harding landed near the bottom. Eugene Trani and David Wilson, who studied the Harding presidency, concluded that he was a “poor president,” a man without vision or moral leadership. Later historians looked more closely. In 1969 Robert K. Murray argued in The Harding Years that Harding had real successes and that his reputation rested too much on rumor. In 2004 John Dean, who wrote a short biography, pointed to Harding’s record on civil rights as a reason to look again. Others take a balanced view, crediting him with some achievements and blaming him for weak judgment in choosing friends. These historians are not only arguing about facts. They are also choosing different ways to judge a president: by results in office, by vision and leadership, or by personal honesty and the choices of the people he appointed.

Five voices will let you test these views. They include his own inaugural address, a sharp newspaper column about his speaking style, a speech Harding gave in Birmingham, Alabama, about race, a senator’s report on Teapot Dome, and the memory of Herbert Hoover, who served in Harding’s cabinet. As you read, remember that the writers had different reasons for writing, that some wrote in the middle of events and others looked back years later, and that critics and defenders often judged Harding by different standards. Ask of each source: whose argument does this evidence help most?

DEBATE 1 · CASE FILES 1–3

Entering the Great War: Security, Money, or Duty?

The debate:

Why did the United States enter the Great War in 1917: to protect its national security, to protect its economic interests, or to defend democracy and a moral cause?

  • ★ NATIONAL SECURITY: Arthur Link and Ross Kennedy argued that German submarine warfare and the Zimmermann telegram threatened American security; critics point out that the British blockade also broke neutral rights.
  • ◆ ECONOMIC SELF-INTEREST: Charles Beard and Hartley Grattan argued that loans and trade ties to the Allies pushed the country into war.
  • ● MORAL DUTY: Ernest May argued that Wilson saw a duty to defend democracy, the idea behind “make the world safe for democracy.”
1917Case file 1 of 8
Who: President Woodrow Wilson
From: War message to Congress, April 2, 1917

The president asks Congress to declare war on Germany.

The present German submarine warfare against commerce is a warfare against mankind. It is a war against all nations. . . . The world must be made safe for democracy. Its peace must be planted upon the tested foundations of political liberty. We have no selfish ends to serve. We desire no conquest, no dominion. We seek no indemnities for ourselves, no material compensation for the sacrifices we shall freely make. We are but one of the champions of the rights of mankind.
Woodrow Wilson, “Address by the President of the United States,” April 2, 1917, Congressional Record, 65th Cong., 1st Sess., 1917, 55:102–104, text from Teaching American History, Ashbrook Center.
Word help: commerce = trade · dominion = rule over other lands · indemnities = payments to cover the costs of war · champions = defenders

1917Case file 2 of 8
Who: Senator Robert La Follette
From: Speech in the U.S. Senate against the war resolution, April 4, 1917

A senator argues that neutrality was not kept fairly and that war is the result.

The failure to treat the belligerent nations of Europe alike, the failure to reject the unlawful “war zones” of both Germany and Great Britain is wholly accountable for our present dilemma. We should not seek to hide our blunder behind the smoke of battle to inflame the mind of our people by half truths into the frenzy of war in order that they may never appreciate the real cause of it until it is too late. I do not believe that our national honor is served by such a course. The right way is the honorable way.
Robert M. La Follette, speech in the Senate, April 4, 1917, Congressional Record–Senate, 224–225, text from History Matters (Hanover College copy).
Word help: belligerent = a nation at war · dilemma = a hard choice with no good answer · blunder = a bad mistake · frenzy = wild excitement

1917Case file 3 of 8
Who: Senator George Norris
From: Speech in the U.S. Senate against the war resolution, April 4, 1917

A senator says that loans to the Allies have shaped public feeling for war.

We have loaned many hundreds of millions of dollars to the allies in this controversy. While such action was legal and countenanced by international law, there is no doubt in my mind but the enormous amount of money loaned to the allies in this country has been instrumental in bringing about a public sentiment in favor of our country taking a course that would make every bond worth a hundred cents on the dollar and making the payment of every debt certain and sure.
George W. Norris, speech in the Senate, April 4, 1917, Congressional Record, 65th Cong., 1st Sess., Vol. LV, pt. I, 212–213, text from the MIT 21H.102 course page.
Word help: countenanced = approved · instrumental = important in causing something · sentiment = public feeling · bond = a loan paper that promises repayment

DEBATE 2 · CASE FILES 4–8

Assessing Warren G. Harding: Poor President or Underrated?

The debate:

Was Warren G. Harding a poor president whose scandals and weak leadership failed the country, or an underrated president whose record deserves a second look?

  • ★ POOR PRESIDENT: In 1948 Arthur Schlesinger Sr. ranked Harding near the bottom, and Eugene Trani and David Wilson called him a “poor president” who lacked vision and moral leadership.
  • ◆ UNDERRATED: Robert K. Murray (1969) and John Dean (2004) argued that Harding’s record, including his civil rights record, deserves more credit than his reputation gives it.
  • ● BOTH / MIXED: A source that fits both arguments: it shows real strengths and real failures at once.
1921Case file 4 of 8
Who: President Warren G. Harding
From: Inaugural address, March 4, 1921

The new president tells Americans what they should expect from their government.

Our most dangerous tendency is to expect too much of government, and at the same time do for it too little. We contemplate the immediate task of putting our public household in order. . . . We must face a condition of grim reality, charge off our losses and start afresh. . . . I would like government to do all it can to mitigate; then, in understanding, in mutuality of interest, in concern for the common good, our tasks will be solved. No altered system will work a miracle. Any wild experiment will only add to the confusion. Our best assurance lies in efficient administration of our proven system.
Warren G. Harding, Inaugural Address, March 4, 1921, National Archives; text from the Avalon Project, Yale Law School.
Word help: tendency = a habit or leaning · mitigate = make less severe · mutuality = shared, two-way · efficient = working well without waste

1921Case file 5 of 8
Who: H. L. Mencken, Baltimore journalist
From: Newspaper column, “Gamalielese,” The Evening Sun (Baltimore), March 7, 1921

A sharp-tongued critic reviews the new president’s inaugural speech.

[H]e writes the worst English I have ever encountered. It reminds me of a string of wet sponges; . . . it reminds me of stale bean soup, of college yells, of dogs barking idiotically through endless nights. It is so bad that a sort of grandeur creeps into it. . . . A tight fabric of ideas would weary and exasperate the audience; what it wants is a simple loud burble of words, a procession of phrases that roar, a series of whoops.
H. L. Mencken, “Gamalielese,” The Evening Sun (Baltimore), March 7, 1921, p. 10, as printed in the Norton exercise (Norton’s page is titled “On Balder and Dash”).
Word help: idiotically = in a silly way · grandeur = great impressiveness · exasperate = make very annoyed · burble = a bubbling, meaningless flow of sound

1921Case file 6 of 8
Who: President Warren G. Harding
From: Speech, “The South and the Negro,” Birmingham, Alabama, October 26, 1921

The president speaks about race in a Southern city.

Politically and economically there need be no occasion for great and permanent differentiation, for limitations of the individual’s opportunity, provided that on both sides there shall be recognition of the absolute divergence in things social and racial. . . . Men of both races may well stand uncompromisingly against every suggestion of social equality. . . . I would say let the black man vote when he is fit to vote: prohibit the white man voting when he is unfit to vote. . . . On the other hand I would insist upon equal educational opportunity for both.
Warren G. Harding, “The South and the Negro,” Birmingham, Alabama, October 26, 1921, as printed in The New York Times, October 27, 1921, pp. 1, 11, and in the Norton exercise. The words “black” and “Negro” are the source’s.

Who: Senator Gerald P. Nye of North Dakota
From: Report on the Teapot Dome investigation, Congressional Record, 1928

A senator reports what the Senate found about the leasing of the Navy’s oil reserves.

The investigation thus far discloses that the leasing of the naval oil reserves constitutes criminal conspiracy against the rights, interests, and properties of the United States Government unparalleled in the history of this or any other civilized nation. . . . Who: Herbert Hoover, former president and Harding’s Secretary of Commerce
From: Memoir, The Memoirs of Herbert Hoover: The Cabinet and the Presidency, 1920–1933

A member of Harding’s cabinet looks back on the man and his presidency.

President Harding died in August, 1923, after two and one-half years in office. He was a kind of dual personality. . . . He had real quality in geniality, in good will and in ability for pleasing address. He was not a man with either the experience or the intellectual quality that the position needed. But he was neither a “reactionary” nor a “radical.” . . . [A]ny objective weighing of Mr. Harding’s Presidential contribution in the balances of time must show that his playmates tipped the scales from his very considerable accomplishments in national progress toward national degeneration.
