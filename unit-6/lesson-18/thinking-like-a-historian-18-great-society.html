Part 1 · Background Reading

What Should Washington Do About Poverty?

By the early 1960s the United States was the richest nation in history, yet millions of Americans lived in poverty. Many of them were rural families, older people, and Black Americans in cities and the South who had been left out of the postwar boom. After President John F. Kennedy was assassinated in November 1963, Lyndon B. Johnson became president and promised to carry on Kennedy’s agenda. In January 1964 he declared an “unconditional war on poverty,” and Congress soon passed the Economic Opportunity Act, which created job training, a Job Corps, and community action programs for poor neighborhoods.

On May 22, 1964, Johnson spoke to graduates at the University of Michigan and gave his program a name: the Great Society. He said it began with an end to poverty and racial injustice and would be built in cities, in the countryside, and in classrooms. After the 1964 election, in which he won by a landslide and his Democratic Party held large majorities in Congress, lawmakers passed Medicare and Medicaid for health care, the first major federal aid for schools, and the Voting Rights Act. Supporters saw these laws as a promise that the richest country could and should give everyone a fair chance.

Not everyone agreed. Two years before Johnson spoke, the economist Milton Friedman of the University of Chicago published Capitalism and Freedom. He was not an enemy of all government; he thought it should keep order, enforce contracts, and protect people from outside enemies. But he warned that power gathered in political hands was a danger to liberty, and he believed that competitive markets, not federal programs, produced prosperity and freedom. Many conservatives and libertarians shared this outlook. They argued that big federal programs wasted money, weakened local decision-making, and could make people depend on government.

The argument did not end in the 1960s. In January 2014, on the fiftieth anniversary of Johnson’s declaration, the Council of Economic Advisers, a group of economists who advise the president and who then worked for Barack Obama, published a report about the War on Poverty. It argued that poverty had fallen when measured to include benefits such as tax credits and food assistance, which the government’s usual poverty line leaves out, while admitting that millions of people remained poor. Critics of such arguments say that how poverty is measured is itself part of the debate, and that the report came from a government that was defending its own record.

Today, writers and politicians who lean toward active government and those who lean toward limited government still use the Great Society as evidence. One side points to programs like Medicare and the fall in measured poverty; the other points to the limits of federal power and the risks of dependence. In this debate, you will read a conservative thinker, the president who started the program, and a later government report. Ask which view each one helps, and remember that Johnson’s own speech called for cooperation between national and local leaders, so some sources help both.

1962Case file 1 of 3
Who: Milton Friedman, economist at the University of Chicago
From: Capitalism and Freedom (1962)

An economist explains why he thinks government power should be kept small.

Freedom is a rare and delicate plant. Our minds tell us, and history confirms, that the great threat to freedom is the concentration of power. Government is necessary to preserve our freedom, it is an instrument through which we can exercise our freedom; yet by concentrating power in political hands, it is also a threat to freedom. . . . First, the scope of government must be limited. Its major function must be to protect our freedom both from the enemies outside our gates and from our fellow-citizens: to preserve law and order, to enforce private contracts, to foster competitive markets.
Milton Friedman, Introduction, Capitalism and Freedom (Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 1962), 1–4.
Word help: concentration of power = power gathered in a few hands · scope = range · private contracts = agreements between individuals or businesses · competitive markets = markets where many sellers compete

1964Case file 2 of 3
Who: President Lyndon B. Johnson
From: Commencement address at the University of Michigan, May 22, 1964

A president tells graduates what he wants the nation to build.

The Great Society rests on abundance and liberty for all. It demands an end to poverty and racial injustice, to which we are totally committed in our time. But that is just the beginning. . . . So I want to talk to you today about three places where we begin to build the Great Society--in our cities, in our countryside, and in our classrooms. . . . The solution to these problems does not rest on a massive program in Washington, nor can it rely solely on the strained resources of local authority. They require us to create new concepts of cooperation, a creative federalism, between the National Capital and the leaders of local communities.
Lyndon B. Johnson, “Remarks at the University of Michigan,” May 22, 1964, American Presidency Project (presidency.ucsb.edu), which reprints Public Papers of the Presidents: Lyndon B. Johnson, 1963–64, Book I.
Word help: abundance = plenty · federalism = the sharing of power between national and state or local governments · National Capital = Washington, D.C. · strained = stretched thin

2014Case file 3 of 3
Who: The Council of Economic Advisers, a group of economists who advise President Barack Obama
From: The War on Poverty 50 Years Later: A Progress Report, January 2014

Fifty years after Johnson’s declaration, the White House counts what has changed.

Poverty has declined by more than one-third since 1967. The percent of the population in poverty when measured to include tax credits and other benefits has declined from 25.8 percent in 1967 to 16.0 percent in 2012. . . . Despite real progress in the War on Poverty, there is more work to do. In 2012, there were 49.7 million Americans grappling with the economic and social hardships of living below the poverty line, including 13.4 million children.
Council of Economic Advisers, The War on Poverty 50 Years Later: A Progress Report (Washington, DC: Executive Office of the President, January 2014).
Word help: declined = gone down · tax credits = reductions in taxes for certain people · grappling = struggling · poverty line = income level below which a family counts as poor
