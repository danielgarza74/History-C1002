Part 1 · Background Reading

How Did the Vietnam War End?

American troops entered Vietnam in large numbers in the mid-1960s to keep Communist North Vietnam from taking over South Vietnam, and by 1968 more than half a million were fighting there. More than 58,000 Americans died in the war, and it divided the nation. Antiwar protests spread on college campuses, many Americans doubted what their leaders told them, and the Tet Offensive of early 1968 shook confidence that the war was being won. Richard Nixon, elected president that year, promised to end the war and win what he called “peace with honor.”

Nixon’s plan was “Vietnamization,” which meant gradually withdrawing American troops while South Vietnam’s army took over the fighting, supported by American aid and bombing. In 1970 he sent troops into Cambodia, which set off new protests at home. His national security adviser, Henry Kissinger, held secret talks with the North Vietnamese negotiator Le Duc Tho in Paris. When talks stalled in December 1972, Nixon ordered intense bombing of Hanoi and Haiphong, and soon afterward the two sides agreed on a settlement. South Vietnam’s president, Nguyen Van Thieu, accepted it only reluctantly, after the United States promised continued support. On January 23, 1973, Nixon announced on television and radio that an agreement had been reached, and the Paris Peace Accords were signed four days later. Under the agreement, American troops left, American prisoners of war came home, and a cease-fire began. But North Vietnamese troops were allowed to stay in the South, and the fighting between North and South Vietnam soon resumed.

Nixon was by then weakened by the Watergate scandal, and Congress, tired of the war, limited further American action. In 1974 and 1975 Congress cut military aid to South Vietnam. On April 10, 1975, as North Vietnamese forces advanced, President Gerald Ford asked Congress for more help, and Kissinger, who was now Secretary of State, held a briefing at the White House to argue that cutting off aid would cause South Vietnam to collapse. A Gallup poll that spring found that about 80 percent of Americans wanted military support to end. Saigon fell on April 30, 1975.

Historians disagree about the 1973 agreement. Nixon and his supporters argued that it was a victory for American goals, and that South Vietnam fell only because Congress later cut off the support the agreement depended on. Jeffrey Kimball and Jeremi Suri argue that the agreement was a way to leave Vietnam while preserving American credibility. They say it was designed to give South Vietnam a “decent interval,” a period of time between the American exit and its fall, so that the United States would not be blamed. Larry Berman argues that Nixon and Kissinger, instead, saw it as a sham peace and a pretext to resume the war with American airpower if North Vietnam violated it. Their disagreement is partly about what Nixon and Kissinger privately intended and partly about whether South Vietnam could ever have survived on its own. As you read each source, ask which view it helps most, and remember that some sources help both.

1973Case file 1 of 3
Who: President Richard Nixon
From: Address to the nation announcing the agreement on ending the war in Vietnam, January 23, 1973

A president tells the country that the long war is over.

I have asked for this radio and television time tonight for the purpose of announcing that we today have concluded an agreement to end the war and bring peace with honor in Vietnam and in Southeast Asia. . . . Throughout the years of negotiations, we have insisted on peace with honor. . . . Now that we have achieved an honorable agreement, let us be proud that America did not settle for a peace that would have betrayed our allies, that would have abandoned our prisoners of war, or that would have ended the war for us but would have continued the war for the 50 million people of Indochina.
Richard Nixon, “Address to the Nation Announcing Conclusion of an Agreement on Ending the War and Restoring Peace in Vietnam,” January 23, 1973, American Presidency Project (presidency.ucsb.edu).
Word help: concluded = finished · honorable = with dignity · betrayed = broken faith with · Indochina = Vietnam, Laos, and Cambodia

1974Case file 2 of 3
Who: Anthony Lewis, columnist for The New York Times
From: “Peace with Honor: II,” The New York Times, June 6, 1974

A columnist looks back on the agreement a year and a half later.

The reality is different. When we began our full-scale military action in Vietnam, in 1966, there were about 3,000 North Vietnamese troops in the south; in the truce we implicitly accepted the presence of 130,000 to 140,000. So much for “victory.” The Saigon Government would not last a day without us. . . . In short, we are getting more of what took us into Vietnam: the surreptitious manipulation of power, the selling of illusions, the avoidance of hard truth.
Anthony Lewis, “Peace with Honor: II,” The New York Times, June 6, 1974, p. 37 (“Abroad at Home” column), New York Times Archive (nytimes.com).
Word help: implicitly = without saying so directly · truce = an agreement to stop fighting · surreptitious = secret · illusions = false beliefs

1975Case file 3 of 3
Who: Henry Kissinger, Secretary of State and national security adviser
From: White House background briefing, April 10, 1975

With South Vietnam under attack, a top official explains what he thinks will happen if aid stops.

If the United States were to announce what many Congressmen have recommended, that we would stop all military assistance, there are foreigners here who will be able to judge on their own what the foreign perception of this problem would be, but there is no question what the result in Vietnam would be. It would lead to an immediate collapse of the situation under the most chaotic conditions imaginable. . . . there are literally tens of thousands of these people now whose physical existence, as well as that of their families, is tied up with us . . . .
Henry Kissinger, White House background briefing, April 10, 1975, Gerald R. Ford Presidential Library (fordlibrarymuseum.gov), White House Press Releases.
Word help: military assistance = weapons and money for an army · perception = how something is seen · chaotic = disorderly · tied up with = closely connected to
