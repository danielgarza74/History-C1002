Part 1 · Background Reading

Debate 1

Welfare, Work, and the 1996 Reform

Since the New Deal, a program called Aid to Families with Dependent Children (AFDC), created by the Social Security Act of 1935, gave cash to poor families, most of them headed by single mothers. By the 1980s and 1990s critics said that it discouraged work and marriage and trapped families in dependence. Defenders answered that the benefits were small and that jobs, child care, and health insurance were out of reach for many parents. Bill Clinton, a Democrat, promised in his 1992 campaign to “end welfare as we know it.” After Republicans won control of Congress in 1994, the argument grew sharper, and Clinton vetoed two Republican welfare bills before signing a third in 1996.

On January 24, 1995, Clinton described his approach in the State of the Union address. In August 1996 he signed the Personal Responsibility and Work Opportunity Reconciliation Act. The law ended AFDC and replaced it with block grants to the states, called Temporary Assistance for Needy Families (TANF), which gives each state a fixed sum of money and wide freedom to design its own program, set a five-year lifetime limit on most federal benefits, and required many recipients to work. The law applied mainly to poor families with children, and states could spend the money on child care and job training as well as cash. Before the final vote, on July 31, 1996, Representative John Lewis of Georgia, a Democrat and a leader of the civil rights movement, urged the House to reject the bill. Peter Edelman, a Clinton administration official who resigned in protest, wrote in The Atlantic in March 1997 that the law ignored the real situations of poor families. Ron Haskins, a researcher at the Brookings Institution who had helped write the law as a congressional staff member, later defended it in his book Work Over Welfare.

Both sides use the results as evidence. Welfare rolls fell sharply in the late 1990s, and more single mothers went to work, helped by a strong economy. Supporters see proof that the law moved families from welfare toward work and independence. Critics point to parents who found only low-wage jobs or none at all, to the loss of health coverage and the cost of child care, and to families who would have no safety net when the economy turned down. The question for this debate is whether the reform helped poor families move toward independence or abandoned them. As you read each source, ask which argument it helps most, and remember that some sources help both.

Debate 2

Newcomers and the Idea of Assimilation

Americans have argued about immigration since colonial times. In the 1750s Benjamin Franklin worried that German settlers in Pennsylvania would keep their own language and customs. Chinese workers came to California for the Gold Rush and then to build railroads, and in 1878 the California Senate sent a memorial to Congress about the Chinese; in 1882 Congress passed the Chinese Exclusion Act. In the 1880s and 1890s immigration from southern and eastern Europe grew, and in 1891 Henry Cabot Lodge, a Republican congressman from Massachusetts, argued in a national magazine that the country should restrict it. Each time, the questions were similar: would the newcomers learn English, accept American institutions, and become loyal citizens, or would they remain apart?

Behind these arguments was an idea called assimilation, the process by which newcomers take on the language, habits, and loyalties of the society around them. Many Americans pictured the nation as a “melting pot” in which different groups blended together. In 1963 two sociologists, Nathan Glazer and Daniel Patrick Moynihan, published Beyond the Melting Pot, a study of the Jewish, Italian, Irish, Puerto Rican, and Black communities of New York City. They argued that the groups there had not simply melted away. They had changed, but they remained identifiable.

In 1965 Congress ended the old quotas that favored northern Europe, and since then most immigrants have come from Latin America and Asia, so the question has returned. People measure assimilation in different ways, such as learning English, schooling, income, and intermarriage, and which measure a writer chooses can shape the answer. Jason Richwine, a former senior policy analyst at the Heritage Foundation, wrote in the National Review in 2009 that Hispanic assimilation is different from that of earlier European groups. Leo Chavez, a professor of anthropology at the University of California, Irvine, argued in his 2008 book The Latino Threat that commentators who call Latino immigrants a danger to the nation repeat a pattern of alarm directed at earlier groups, such as Germans, Chinese, and southern and eastern Europeans, which later proved unjustified. These two writers are not case files. They are positions in a modern debate. Writers today can use history in different ways: one may stress how the present differs from the past, and another how it repeats it. The case files below are older voices that you can test against them. Ask which argument each source helps most, and remember that some sources help both.

Welfare Reform: Independence or Abandonment?

The debate:

Did the 1996 welfare reform move poor families from dependence toward independence, or did it abandon them?

  • ★ PATH TO INDEPENDENCE: President Bill Clinton and supporters of reform argue that work requirements and time limits lift people from dependence to independence.
  • ◆ ABANDONMENT: Representative John Lewis and other critics argue that ending the national guarantee abandons poor families, especially children.
  • ● BOTH / MIXED: A source whose evidence fits parts of both arguments (mixed), or does not fit either one cleanly.
1995Case file 1 of 8
Who: President Bill Clinton
From: State of the Union address, January 24, 1995

A president lays out what he wants welfare to be.

We have to make welfare what it was meant to be, a second chance, not a way of life. We have to help those on welfare move to work as quickly as possible, to provide child care and teach them skills, if that's what they need, for up to 2 years. And after that, there ought to be a simple, hard rule: Anyone who can work must go to work. . . . But our goal must be to liberate people and lift them up from dependence to independence, from welfare to work, from mere childbearing to responsible parenting. Our goal should not be to punish them because they happen to be poor.
William J. Clinton, “Address Before a Joint Session of the Congress on the State of the Union,” January 24, 1995, Public Papers of the Presidents of the United States: William J. Clinton, 1995, vol. 1, via govinfo.gov.

1755Case file 5 of 8
Who: Benjamin Franklin, a Pennsylvania printer and writer
From: “Observations Concerning the Increase of Mankind” (written 1751, published 1755)

A leading colonist worries about German settlers in Pennsylvania.

[W]hy should the Palatine Boors [Germans] be suffered to swarm into our Settlements, and by herding together establish their Language and Manners to the Exclusion of ours?
