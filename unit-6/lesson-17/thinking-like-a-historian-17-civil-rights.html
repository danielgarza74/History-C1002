Part 1 · Background Reading

Who Moved the Struggle for Civil Rights?

In the early 1950s, segregation was the law in much of the South. Black and white students attended separate schools, rode in separate sections of buses, and used separate public facilities, and in many places Black citizens were kept from voting by taxes, tests, and threats. Since 1896 the Supreme Court had allowed this under the idea of “separate but equal.” The National Association for the Advancement of Colored People (NAACP) had spent years challenging that idea through lawsuits, and in 1954 it won. In Brown v. Board of Education of Topeka, the Court ruled unanimously, in an opinion written by Chief Justice Earl Warren, that segregated public schools were unconstitutional.

Southern leaders pushed back. In March 1956, about one hundred members of Congress from Southern states signed a statement known as the Southern Manifesto, which attacked the decision and encouraged resistance. In September 1957, Governor Orval Faubus of Arkansas used the state’s National Guard to keep nine Black students from entering Central High School in Little Rock, and an angry crowd gathered outside. President Dwight D. Eisenhower, who did not want to be seen as a champion of integration, answered with Executive Order 10730 on September 24. It sent federal troops to Little Rock and placed the Arkansas National Guard under federal command. The nine students entered the school under military guard.

Other Americans were already acting on their own. In February 1960, four Black college students sat down at a whites-only lunch counter in Greensboro, North Carolina, and sit-ins spread across the South. Ella Baker, a veteran organizer who worked for the Southern Christian Leadership Conference, helped students gather at Shaw University in Raleigh that April. The meeting led to the Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee (SNCC), and Baker wrote about it for a newspaper, the Southern Patriot. In Mississippi, SNCC workers helped local people register to vote. One was Fannie Lou Hamer, a sharecropper from Ruleville. On August 31, 1962, she and others rode to the courthouse in Indianola to try to register. She was fired from her plantation job that same day and later suffered a brutal beating in jail after returning from a voter-registration workshop in 1963. In 1964 she helped found the Mississippi Freedom Democratic Party, which challenged the all-white state delegation at the Democratic National Convention in Atlantic City, and on August 22 she testified about her experiences before the convention’s Credentials Committee.

Historians disagree about where the movement’s power came from. In 1977 Carl Brauer called the era a Second Reconstruction in which the national government, through presidents, Congress, and the courts, made change possible. Later historians, including John Dittmer in his 1994 book on Mississippi, Local People, Belinda Robnett in 2000, and Clayborne Carson, with his emphasis on a broader “Black Freedom Struggle,” stressed that local men, women, and students started and sustained the movement, and that they pushed the government to act. As you read each source, ask whether it shows Washington enabling change, local people enabling change, or the two working together, and remember that some sources help both.

1954Case file 1 of 5
Who: Chief Justice Earl Warren, writing for a unanimous U.S. Supreme Court
From: Brown v. Board of Education of Topeka, May 17, 1954

The nation’s highest court answers a question brought by Black families about their children’s schools.

We come then to the question presented: does segregation of children in public schools solely on the basis of race, even though the physical facilities and other “tangible” factors may be equal, deprive the children of the minority group of equal educational opportunities? We believe that it does. . . . We conclude that, in the field of public education, the doctrine of “separate but equal” has no place. Separate educational facilities are inherently unequal. Therefore, we hold that the plaintiffs and others similarly situated for whom the actions have been brought are, by reason of the segregation complained of, deprived of the equal protection of the laws guaranteed by the Fourteenth Amendment.
Brown v. Board of Education of Topeka, 347 U.S. 483 (1954), opinion of the Court, via Justia.
Word help: segregation = forced separation of people by race · tangible = physical, countable · doctrine = a rule or principle · plaintiffs = people who bring a lawsuit

1956Case file 2 of 5
Who: Nineteen U.S. senators and eighty-two U.S. representatives from the South
From: “Declaration of Constitutional Principles” (the Southern Manifesto), March 12, 1956

Members of Congress from the South answer the Supreme Court’s school decision.

The unwarranted decision of the Supreme Court in the public school cases is now bearing the fruit always produced when men substitute naked power for established law. . . . The original Constitution does not mention education. Neither does the Fourteenth Amendment nor any other amendment. . . . This unwarranted exercise of power by the Court, contrary to the Constitution, is creating chaos and confusion in the states principally affected. It is destroying the amicable relations between the white and Negro races that have been created through ninety years of patient effort by the good people of both races.
“Declaration of Constitutional Principles,” Congressional Record, 84th Congress, 2nd session, March 12, 1956, via Teaching American History.
Word help: unwarranted = not justified · naked power = power with no legal reason · amicable = friendly · Negro = the period word for Black people

A president orders the military into a state after a governor blocks nine students from a school.

I, DWIGHT D. 1960Case file 4 of 5
Who: Ella Baker, a civil rights organizer
From: “Bigger Than a Hamburger,” Southern Patriot, May 1960

An organizer writes about a meeting of student protesters at Shaw University.

The Student Leadership Conference made it crystal clear that current sit-ins and other demonstrations are concerned with something much bigger than a hamburger or even a giant-sized Coke. Whatever may be the difference in approach to their goal, the Negro and white students, North and South, are seeking to rid America of the scourge of racial segregation and discrimination—not only at lunch counters, but in every aspect of life. . . . The students showed willingness to be met on the basis of equality, but were intolerant of anything that smacked of manipulation or domination.

1964Case file 5 of 5
Who: Fannie Lou Hamer, a sharecropper from Ruleville, Mississippi
From: Testimony before the Credentials Committee, Democratic National Convention, August 22, 1964

A Mississippi woman tells a national party committee what happened when she tried to register to vote.

It was the 31st of August in 1962 that eighteen of us traveled twenty-six miles to the county courthouse in Indianola to try to register to become first-class citizens. . . . All of this is on account of we want to register, to become first-class citizens. And if the Freedom Democratic Party is not seated now, I question America. Is this America, the land of the free and the home of the brave, where we have to sleep with our telephones off the hooks because our lives be threatened daily, because we want to live as decent human beings, in America?
Fannie Lou Hamer, testimony before the Credentials Committee, Democratic National Convention, Atlantic City, N.J., August 22, 1964, as transcribed by American RadioWorks, Say It Plain (americanradioworks.publicradio.org); the Norton exercise agrees where the passages overlap.
